1. Executive Summary
The project studies machine-learning-based detection of DNS-related covert communication and data
exfiltration. Conventional DNS (Do53) can expose the queried domain and DNS fields to a network observer.
DNS-over-HTTPS (DoH) carries DNS messages inside encrypted HTTPS connections, hiding query content from
an ordinary passive observer and shifting detection toward observable flow, timing, size, and TLS-related
characteristics.
The research framework compares six classifiers—Logistic Regression (LR), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN),
Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), XGBoost (XGB), and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). Eight
experiments organize the work: two within-dataset baselines, two cross-protocol transfer directions, a
feature-group ablation, top-K feature selection, external benchmark evaluation, and DNSTunnel2026
validation/transfer analysis.
The project combines metric reporting with feature analysis, SHAP explanations, saved run configurations,
held-out predictions, plots, and a dashboard. The important scientific challenge is generalization: high
performance on a random split from one dataset does not prove that the model will work on unseen domains,
sessions, campaigns, capture periods, or different protocols.
Main takeaway The strongest defensible project story is not “every model is perfect.” It is that a
comparative framework is being used to study within-dataset detection, cross-dataset/protocol transfer,
feature relevance, and the limits of random-split evaluation.

2. Problem Statement and Background
2.1 Problem statement
Organizations depend on DNS, so blocking all DNS communication is usually impractical. An attacker may
abuse DNS queries to move encoded fragments of unauthorized data to infrastructure under their control.
DoH creates an additional monitoring challenge because DNS messages are encrypted inside HTTPS. A
detector that relies on readable domain strings may not have access to those strings when monitoring
encrypted traffic.
The problem addressed by this project is to evaluate how effectively machine-learning classifiers can identify
benign versus exfiltration-labelled traffic in conventional DNS and DoH datasets, and to investigate how
detection performance and feature relevance change when the dataset, protocol, or feature representation
changes.

2.3 Why conventional DNS and DoH differ
Figure 2. Observable feature spaces differ between conventional DNS and DoH.
For Do53, lexical signals such as query length, character entropy, label composition, DNS record types, and
TTL-related statistics may be available. For DoH, a passive observer generally cannot inspect the encrypted
DNS query name, so detection often relies on traffic-level, flow-level, temporal, statistical, and observable TLS
characteristics. The exact set depends on the dataset and where traffic was captured.
Important distinction DNS tunneling is a technique; data exfiltration is an objective. Malicious DoH
traffic, DGA-related traffic, and DNS exfiltration are overlapping but not identical labels. Interpret each
benchmark according to its own documented target label.
3. Research Gap, Objectives, Questions, and Hypotheses
3.1 Research gap
The research paper argues that many detection studies report strong results on an individual dataset, often
under a random train/test split. Such scores do not automatically demonstrate robustness across a different
resolver, network, collection period, attack tool, campaign, or protocol. This project is designed to go beyond a
single within-dataset score by comparing models, studying feature groups, testing transfer in both directions,
and inspecting model explanations.
3.2 Objectives
 Build a unified comparative framework for conventional DNS and DoH exfiltration-labelled datasets.
 Compare six established machine-learning classifiers under documented evaluation conditions.
 Measure precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, false-positive rate, and computational cost where
available.
 Investigate whether models trained on one protocol or dataset can transfer to another.
 Assess the role of feature groups and compact top-K feature sets.
 Use feature importance and SHAP to explain what information trained models use.
 Use external benchmarks to provide additional evidence, while respecting their label and feature
definitions.
 Create a reproducible dashboard/report workflow that traces results to saved artifacts.
3.3 Research questions from the paper
 RQ1: How effectively can different ML models detect conventional DNS-based data exfiltration?
 RQ2: How effectively can the same models detect DoH-based data exfiltration?
 RQ3: How well do models trained on conventional DNS exfiltration generalize to DoH exfiltration?
 RQ4: How well do models trained on DoH exfiltration generalize to conventional DNS exfiltration?
 RQ5: Which feature groups remain informative when DNS content becomes encrypted?
 RQ6: Which ML approach gives a useful balance among recall, precision, F1, PR-AUC, FPR, and
computational cost?
 RQ7: Which features contribute most strongly to model decisions, and do they remain stable across
datasets?

4.1 Published counts versus experiment counts
The large counts in the paper describe the published primary datasets. Experiment runs may use smaller
samples—for example, the synchronized E1/E2 runs discussed later target a 15,000-row sample. A run must
report the number of rows loaded, deduplicated, sampled, and retained after preprocessing. A result based on
15,000 sampled rows must not be described as training on every row of a 5.35-million-row dataset.
4.2 Where data may reside
The project notes state that dataset discovery may look outside icsproject, including a sibling directory such as
`..\DNS_Covert_Channel_Project\data\raw\`. Therefore, an absent folder inside icsproject alone does not prove a
dataset is unavailable. The configured discovery paths and actual loaded file paths should be checked and
saved in the run manifest.
4.3 Why feature compatibility matters
The primary UFU CSVs have been reported to share zero exact non-label feature columns. This is not, by itself,
a complete semantic proof that no equivalent measurements can be derived, but it prevents direct matrix
transfer using the existing columns. Features must be comparable in definition, units, observation window
and aggregation level—not merely have similar names.
PCAP limitation The shared E3/E4 diagnostic output reports incomplete DNS-360 PCAP archives. One
output states Do53 is missing 2 of 34 parts and DoH is missing 16 of 66 parts; another checklist describes
11 missing DoH parts. This discrepancy must be reconciled from the actual archive inventory before the
research paper states an exact count.

6. Preprocessing and Feature Engineering
6.1 Preprocessing order
1. Load the exact dataset file and record its source path, file hash where practical, row count and schema.
2. Identify the target column and construct X (input features) and y (labels). Remove the target and any
accidental direct copy of the label from X.
3. Handle duplicates and missing values according to a documented rule; log how many rows are removed
or altered.
4. Split the dataset before learning preprocessing parameters. Use stratification where appropriate for class
proportions; consider group separation when records share a capture, session, domain or campaign.
5. Fit the median imputer, scaler, encoder, resampler and supervised feature selector only on the training
data or within each training fold.
6. Apply the fitted transformations to validation/test data without refitting them.
7. Train the model and evaluate only on the designated held-out data. Preserve split identifiers where
possible.
The recent project notes state that post-split median imputation and RobustScaler are used in the
synchronized E1/E2 workflow. That guards against preprocessing statistics leaking from the test set. It does
not remove leakage already embedded in precomputed context features or caused by related records sharing
the split.

8.1 How AUC can be high while F1 is zero
ROC-AUC assesses ranking over many thresholds; F1 uses predicted classes at one threshold. A model may
assign every positive record a score greater than every negative record, producing a high ROC-AUC, while all
positive scores remain below 0.50. At that threshold the model predicts every record as benign, so recall and
F1 for the positive class are zero. In an actual result, label encoding, probability-column selection, score
orientation, alignment and the confusion matrix must also be checked before accepting that interpretation.

10.3 Why transfer may fail
The diagnostic notes describe an observation-unit mismatch: Do53 UDP flows may represent short
query/response transactions, while DoH TCP/TLS sessions may remain open longer and multiplex multiple
DNS messages. A raw five-tuple flow duration or packet-size measurement may therefore measure different
behavior in the two cases. A defensible primary test needs consistent observation windows, time definitions,
feature units, and ground-truth timestamp alignment.
10.4 E4 and high AUC with zero F1
Several E4 runs were reported with F1=0 while RF ROC-AUC varied across artifacts (one report showed about
0.9978; a later screenshot showed 0.9564). Other E4 models also showed F1=0 with different AUC values.
Because run outputs differ, do not consolidate these numbers into a final model-comparison table until each
value is joined to its exact run ID, predictions and configuration. Inspect the score direction, positive class,
probability column, threshold, and confusion matrix.
Defensible conclusion for E3/E4 Current E3/E4 proxy outputs are diagnostic evidence about a particular
flow representation. They do not establish primary UFU-Do53 and UFU-DoH generalization. The primary
transfer claim remains unresolved until compatible, consistently derived primary features are available.
11. E5 and E6 — Feature Studies
11.1 E5: Feature-group ablation
Ablation removes one feature group at a time and compares performance with a baseline. The research
design proposes comparisons such as lexical-only, protocol fields, domain context, heuristics, flow-only,
temporal/common features where the dataset supports them. The goal is to estimate which feature families
are useful to the fitted model.
If F1 does not change after removing a group, possible interpretations include redundancy among groups, one
remaining feature group being sufficient, an insensitive test setup, or an implementation problem that failed
to remove the requested columns. Verify the actual selected column list for every run. A performance drop
should never be written into the report unless the measured result shows it.

E6 compares a full feature set with compact subsets such as the top 10, 15 or 20 features. The intended trade-

new model and use the same untouched test split.

13. Explainability: Feature Importance, SHAP, and Plots
13.1 What SHAP means
SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) attributes parts of a model output to individual input features. A local
explanation describes one prediction; a global summary aggregates contributions across a set of records.
Mean absolute SHAP values show the average magnitude of a feature’s contribution, not whether high values
push a prediction toward benign or exfiltration.

13.3 Why native feature gain and SHAP can differ
XGBoost native gain summarizes improvement associated with tree splits during training. SHAP summarizes
feature contributions to predictions on the explained data. They are different measures and may use different
data partitions. Correlated features can share or exchange importance, so rank differences are not
automatically a contradiction.

15. Threats to Validity and Scientific Integrity
15.1 Data leakage
Data leakage occurs when information unavailable at prediction time influences the trained model or
evaluation. It can happen through direct target leakage, identifiers or metadata that reveal labels, fitting
preprocessing on the whole dataset, feature selection using test labels, duplicate records across the split, or
correlated examples from the same source group. Correctly fitting a scaler on train-only data addresses only
one subset of these risks.
15.2 Group-separated evaluation
Random-row splits can put examples from the same domain, campaign, session, capture or generator in both
training and testing. A group-separated split holds out complete groups, which better tests whether the model
transfers to new sources of variation. The grouping key must match the scientific claim: unseen domains,
sessions, campaigns and future time periods are related but not identical generalization tests.

15.3 Data provenance and synthetic data
Synthetic data can test whether loaders, APIs, charts and experiment runners function. It cannot establish that

packet captures, synthetic, or a proxy constructed from another benchmark. Synthetic rows or labels must
never be presented as genuine external benchmark samples.
15.4 Small benchmark samples
The reported CIRA L2 test set has 199 flows in one evaluation, and the reported CIC-Bell stateless test set has
200. Metrics from small test sets can vary substantially with a small number of errors. The report should
include class counts, confusion matrices, and uncertainty analysis where feasible. Deduplication should be
described, and related records should not cross the split unnoticed.

17.2 Likely viva questions
Question Answer
Why use DNS for exfiltration? DNS is widely needed, so it can be attractive to abuse as a covert
path for encoded data.
Why is DoH harder to inspect? DNS messages are carried inside HTTPS, hiding the query payload
from a passive observer without decryption.
Why compare six classifiers? They represent different model families and provide a broader
performance/complexity comparison.
Why report PR-AUC as well as ROC-AUC? PR-AUC is often more informative when the positive class is rare.
Can F1 be 1.0? Yes, on a particular test set. It does not prove universal
generalization or rule out leakage.
Why can ROC-AUC be high but F1=0? Good score ranking can coexist with a threshold that predicts no
positive examples; also check label and score alignment.
Why is E3 diagnostic only? The current proxy features originate from different external traffic
sources, not matched flow windows from both primary UFU
datasets.
What does E5 do? Removes feature groups to measure how model performance
changes.
What does E6 do? Compares full and top-K feature sets to study
compactness/performance trade-offs.
What does SHAP tell us? How the fitted model attributes contributions to predictions on the
explained samples; it does not prove causality.
Why are datasets imbalanced? Benign records substantially outnumber exfiltration records in
both primary datasets.
What is the biggest limitation? Generalization and feature compatibility; random-split results can
exploit dataset-specific patterns.
Does a CIRA result prove exfiltration detection? Only if the benchmark label/task corresponds to the exfiltration
claim; malicious DoH and exfiltration are not identical.
Why can E8 be partly completed? Within-dataset train/test evaluation may be possible while
primary-to-DNSTunnel transfer is blocked by feature
incompatibility.
What makes an experiment reproducible? Traceable inputs, labels, split indices, seed, configuration,
predictions, metrics and artifact provenance.
